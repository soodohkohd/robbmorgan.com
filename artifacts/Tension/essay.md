# Tension

*by Robb Morgan*

## 1. Sunset

Everybody stops for a sunset.

I've watched it happen my whole life. People who couldn't agree on the weather will stand on the same stretch of beach, phones up, not saying a word. Nobody had to be convinced. Nobody was told whether it was beautiful or not.

Why? Because it's the moment a hard day meets a quiet night. Or, at the other end, a rough night meets the chance of a new day. Neither one wins. For a few minutes you get both, and somehow that's the best part of the whole twenty-four hours.

That isn't just me being sentimental. Researchers at the University of Exeter showed more than 2,500 people the same scenes at different times of day. The sunrise and sunset versions were rated more beautiful and more awe-inspiring than any other hour, and people said they'd pay more to visit a place at sunrise than under a clear blue sky.

Here's the part I love. When you watch the sun sitting on the horizon, it's already gone. You're only seeing it because the air bends its light so you can still see it. And the color? A NOAA forecaster named Stephen Corfidi put it about as well as anybody can: sunsets are red because the daytime sky is blue. All day long the air scatters blue light across the sky. When the sun gets low, its light has to cross so much more air that the blue gets scattered out of the way, and what's left to reach you is orange and red.

So the most beautiful thing most of us see on any given day is made of the day and the night at the same time. You don't get one without the other.

Now picture a rope.

Two teams, one rope, a line in the dirt. Every person on that rope feels exactly one thing... the other side dragging them toward the line. They're leaning back, digging in, and they're sure their end is where all the effort is.

The rope feels both. Every inch of it is being pulled in two directions at once. That's what tension is. And here's the thing about it. Let go of either end and the tension doesn't move to the other side. It's gone, all the way along. The rope drops in the dirt, and whoever's still holding on lands flat on their back.

We've been taught that the passion lives at the ends. Pick a team. Dig in. Pull harder. The people in the middle are the ones who "don't care" or "can't make up their minds."

I think we've got that backwards.

So I'm going to give the middle a name. There's the lowercase kind of tension, the knot in your stomach before a hard conversation. We've all got plenty of that. Tension with a capital T is something else. It's the place where both pulls are felt at the same time, and held. It's the sunset. It's the rope. It's where the day meets the night, and where you and the person you're sure is wrong about everything are, like it or not, tied to the same thing.

This essay is about living there. Politics, race, sex, God... all of it. The middle isn't always right, and I'll get to that. But it's the only place where both sides get felt at once.

I know what the ends feel like. I spent years pulling on one of them.

## 2. The Edges Are Easy

In 2006 I started writing an op-ed I never finished. I called it "Republocrats." It opened like this:

> I once morphed a picture of an elephant and a donkey on my computer, and the result was pretty much what I had expected - a picture of a huge ass. Somewhat indicative of today's politicians, if you ask me.

I thought I was pretty funny. I also thought I was above it all. A few lines later I admitted something I didn't want to look at too hard. I said I belonged to "a particular party that may have skewed or tainted my own views and opinions."

That party was the Republican Party. I could see the rope. I knew which end I was on. And I kept pulling anyway.

That says less about me than about people. Picking a side is about the easiest thing a human being does.

In 1970, a psychologist named Henri Tajfel published an experiment he'd run on teenage boys at a school outside Bristol, England. He split them into two groups. In one version, he had them guess at dots flashed on a screen, then told them they were "overestimators" or "underestimators." In another, he told them they preferred the paintings of Paul Klee or the paintings of Wassily Kandinsky. Either way, the groups were basically random. The boys already knew each other. Nothing was at stake.

Then he had each boy hand out small amounts of real money to other boys, known only by a code number and a group. Never to himself.

They favored their own group. Consistently. And sometimes they'd pick the option that made the biggest gap between the two groups, even when a different choice would have given their own side more money. Beating the other group by more mattered more than having more.

Over a painting they'd been told they liked.

Later versions made the groups openly random, and people still favored their own. The effect has been repeated many times since.

If you want to see it in the wild, look at sports. Researchers at Princeton scanned the brains of eighteen die-hard Red Sox and Yankees fans while they watched animated replays of plays. When the rival team failed, the reward center of the brain lit up... even when the rival lost to the Orioles, a team neither group cared about. And the fans whose brains lit up the most tended to be the ones who said they'd be more likely to heckle, threaten or even hit a fan of the other team.

I'm a lifelong Steelers fan, so this next one hits home.

Another research project gave football fans 100 "rivalry points" to spread across the teams they can't stand. Browns fans put more than half of theirs on Pittsburgh. Steelers fans gave the Browns about fifteen. I'd love to tell you that proves something about Cleveland. It doesn't. Ask a Steelers fan about Baltimore.

But here's the part that usually gets left out, and it matters for everything that follows. What Tajfel's boys showed was favoritism, not hate. Decades of research since then say the same thing. The pull toward "us" comes almost free. Getting from "us" to hating "them" takes something more... a threat, a competition, somebody stoking it.

Hold onto that. We'll come back to who's doing the stoking.

Now look at the teams we've got. Democrat or Republican. Black or white. Believer or not. Man or woman. Steelers or Browns. Some of them we picked. Most of them we were born into, or born into the families that handed them to us. Every one of them comes with a rope.

Standing at one end of a rope is simple. You feel one pull. You know exactly which way to lean, and everybody around you is leaning the same way. It feels like standing for something. Most days it's just standing with somebody.

That's why the ends are so crowded.

## 3. We Fight What We Hear

Quick question. If your neighbor went out of town for a week, would you grab their mail?

Most of us would. When Pew asked in 2025, three out of four Americans said they'd bring in a neighbor's mail or water their plants. Now flip it around. Would your neighbor do that for you? Only about half of us thought so.

Think about what that means. Most of us are better neighbors than we give our neighbors credit for. Which means a lot of the people we've quietly written off have written us off the same way, and we're both wrong.

That gap, between who people really are and who we picture, is where a lot of our fights actually live.

In 1951, Princeton and Dartmouth played a football game so rough that Princeton's star left with a broken nose and a concussion, and a Dartmouth player left with a broken leg. Afterward, two psychologists, one from each school, showed students from both campuses the same game film and had them count the fouls. Princeton students counted almost ten Dartmouth fouls. Dartmouth students, watching the exact same film, counted about four.

Same film. Two different games.

It happens with God. Pew once asked Americans to rate religious groups on a warmth scale from 0 to 100. Atheists gave evangelical Christians a 28. White evangelicals gave atheists a 25. Each was the other's coldest score. Two groups about as far apart on God as you can get, and they landed on almost the same number for each other.

Here's the number I can't stop thinking about. People who actually knew an atheist rated atheists a 50. People who didn't know one rated them a 29. Same group being rated. The warmer scores came from the people who had a face to put on it.

It happens between men and women. In Saudi Arabia, researchers privately asked young married men whether women should be allowed to work outside the home. Almost nine in ten said yes. But most of them guessed that fewer of the other men agreed than really did... men from their own neighborhoods, men they knew. When the researchers showed them the real numbers, more of them signed their wives up for a job-matching service, and months later more of those wives had applied for jobs. Nothing changed except what the men believed about each other. That was enough.

And it happens with race. Black Lives Matter started as a hashtag in 2013 and spread across the country in 2014. Before long came the answer: All Lives Matter.

Read those again. Both are true. I've never met anybody who'd argue with the plain words of either one. But nobody was arguing about the plain words. A lot of people on the right heard "your life counts less." A lot of people on the left heard "your problem doesn't count, so change the subject." We spent years answering sentences nobody had actually said.

The problem behind the first slogan is real, and it can be measured. From 2015 through 2024, Black Americans were shot and killed by police at about two and a half times the rate of white Americans, per person. Researchers still argue over how much of that gap is bias and how much is how often people come in contact with police. Those are fair questions. A slogan doesn't answer either one.

I'll confess something. When I started writing this essay, I thought the answer was easy. Drop the first word. Just "Lives Matter." Then it was pointed out to me that "Lives Matter" is just "All Lives Matter" with a fresh coat of paint, and that a lot of the people saying "Black Lives Matter" meant it to end with an unspoken word... *too*.

That's fair. I changed my mind.

We're not that far apart on what we believe. We're miles apart on what we believe about each other.

You know that line on your car's side mirror? Objects in mirror are closer than they appear.

So is the other end of the rope.

## 4. Institutions Drift

In 1973, if you asked Americans which institution they trusted most, the answer was the church. Organized religion topped Gallup's list every year until 1985. About two out of three people had a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in it.

By 2022 it was down to 31%. A big part of the fall came after 2002, when the *Boston Globe* reported on Catholic leaders covering up abuse. By 2020, fewer than half of Americans belonged to a church, synagogue or mosque for the first time in more than eighty years of Gallup asking.

Now look at what didn't fall as far. Belief in God slipped too, from about 98% in the 1950s and '60s to 81% in 2022. That's a real change, and I'm not going to pretend it isn't. But the institution fell about twice as far. And about one in five Americans now calls themselves "spiritual but not religious." They walked out of the building. They didn't stop asking the question.

I'm one of them. I don't trust organized religion. Too much of it, to me, has run on fear, the same way too much of politics does. Do I believe in God? I look around at this planet and see something pretty close to heaven. Then I read about a baby dying of a disease nobody can cure, and I don't know. People have been arguing about that one for a couple thousand years. I'm not going to settle it in an essay.

What I won't do is blame my neighbor for it. Even among Americans with no religion at all, about two out of three say churches and other congregations bring people together, and six in ten say they help the poor. Religious households are more likely to give to charity than households with no religion, 62% to 46%. The woman down the street who prays every night isn't running anything. She's the one bringing a casserole to somebody who's sick.

The institution drifted. She didn't.

I left the Republican Party the same way, in 2008. In the run-up to that election, what I kept seeing from my own side wasn't an argument about Barack Obama's positions. It was about his skin color and his culture. And it got worse after he won. In the summer of 2008, Republicans and Democrats were equally likely to believe the false rumor that Obama was a Muslim, about one in eight in each party. By 2010 it was 31% of Republicans and 10% of Democrats.

Not everybody on my side went along. In October 2008, at a town hall in Minnesota, a woman told John McCain she couldn't trust Obama because "he's an Arab." McCain shook his head and took the microphone back. "No, ma'am," he said. "He's a decent family man, citizen, that I just happen to have disagreements with on fundamental issues." Earlier in that same town hall, his own crowd had booed him for calling Obama a decent person.

That's a man standing in the Tension and paying for it. Not enough people followed him, and I didn't want to be on that end of the rope anymore.

So I let go. But I didn't run to the other end and grab on, either. I see plenty wrong with the Democrats. Different flaws, maybe, but flaws all the same. Jumping from one leaky boat into another isn't getting out of the water. Neither party had earned my loyalty, so neither one got it.

I'm not the first to feel that way, from either direction. Ronald Reagan was a Democrat until 1962. Years later he liked to say, "I didn't leave my party; my party left me." He wasn't the only one. By 1986, Reagan himself was calling the line a cliché. That year he told a story about Chicago's former top cop, who'd switched parties to run for sheriff, saying it to him at the White House. Reagan's answer: "Jim, join the crowd."

Same sentence, opposite direction.

Trust in each other has slipped over the years too. But the steepest drop is at the top, in the institutions farthest from your street. Four out of five Americans trust charities to act in society's best interest. About three in ten trust the federal government. Trust in local government is about where it was in the 1970s.

The closer something is to the people it serves, the more we trust it. That isn't an accident. It's a scoreboard.

Institutions earn trust or lose it. A lot of them lost it fair and square.

## 5. Check Your Map

Here's me, on paper.

I believe trans people deserve the same dignity as anybody else, and I believe women's sports should stay closed to male bodies until science proves the advantage is gone. I don't trust organized religion, and I can't rule out God.

Plot that on a left-right line and I'm all over the place. A conservative would call me a liberal. A liberal would call me a conservative. Somebody in the middle would probably just call me confused.

Now run every one of those through three rules: **People first. Facts over tribe. Institutions earn trust or lose it.**

People first means a trans person's dignity isn't up for a vote. Facts over tribe means the sports question gets settled by evidence, not by which team I'm on. Show me the proof that the advantage is gone and I'll change my mind. I've said that out loud, and I mean it.

The church lost me as an institution. The question of God didn't go anywhere.

Same three rules, every time. I'm not contradictory. I'm just not on your map.

I'm also not unusual. In 2026, Pew Research sorted Americans into nine political groups. About six in ten landed in groups Pew describes as politically mixed, people who don't line up with either party down the line. Only about four in ten fit neatly toward one end or the other.

Back in 2018, a study called *Hidden Tribes* put two statements in front of Americans. "Hate speech is a problem in our country." 82% agreed. "Political correctness is a problem in our country." 80% agreed. Do the math. An awful lot of people said yes to both. On a left-right map, that's a contradiction. In a real human being, it's just paying attention.

You want contradictory? Look at the ends of the rope.

In 2006, under George W. Bush, Pew asked whether it was acceptable for the government to secretly listen in on calls and read emails of terror suspects without a court's approval. 75% of Republicans said yes. Only 36% of Democrats did. In 2013, under Barack Obama, Pew asked about the government using secret court orders to track the phone records of millions of Americans. Now 64% of Democrats said yes, and Republicans dropped to 52%.

To be fair, those weren't the same program. One skipped the courts. The other swept up records on millions of people. You could make an honest case that either one was worse. But look at how the numbers moved. Support swung toward whichever party held the White House, on both sides.

A psychologist named Geoffrey Cohen ran a sharper test on college students. He showed liberals and conservatives a welfare plan and told them which party supported it. Liberals backed a harsh plan if they were told Democrats were for it. Conservatives backed a generous one if they were told Republicans were for it. Then they said the party label had barely mattered to them. In a later round, liberal students said it had mattered a lot more to other people, and the most to Republicans.

That's not a principle. That's a jersey.

I'm not knocking the people who wear it. I wore one for years. That's the pull of the ends: you make one decision, which team, and the team makes the rest of them for you. It's comfortable. It saves a lot of thinking.

The middle doesn't let you off that easy. You have to take every question one at a time, using the same rules, and live with landing on the "wrong" side of somebody's map now and then.

So if I look contradictory to you, check your map.

It might be the map that's wrong.

## 6. The Hand Over the Wall

I joined the Air Force in 1994 and served until 2002. I was Security Police, the part of the Air Force trained to fight on the ground and defend the base. Our ground combat training was run by the Army at Fort Dix, New Jersey, with Air Force instructors on every team.

Almost all of that training came down to one thing: you can't do it alone. Every task, every exercise, every mission was built so that you had to rely on the people next to you.

When I needed help getting over a wall, I didn't care what color the hand was. When I helped somebody out of a "hot" spot, I didn't care which God they prayed to. There wasn't time to care. There was only the wall.

The Air Force figured that out early. When President Truman ordered equal treatment in the armed forces in 1948, the Air Force moved faster and further than any other branch, starting in 1949. Decades later, researchers took advantage of the fact that the Air Force Academy assigns cadets to squadrons at random. White men who happened to land in a squadron with Black cadets who'd been strong students in high school were more likely to pick a Black roommate the next year. Nobody gave them a speech. They just spent a year needing each other.

You don't have to wear a uniform for this to work.

In England, researchers recruited Manchester United fans for what they thought was a study about being a fan. On the walk to another building, a jogger slipped, fell and grabbed his ankle in pain. He was in on it. When he wore a Manchester United shirt, almost every fan stopped to help. When he wore a Liverpool shirt, their bitter rival, most of them walked right past.

Then the researchers ran it again, but first got the fans thinking about themselves as football fans in general. This time they stopped for the Liverpool fan too. The man in a plain shirt, with no team at all? Mostly still walked past.

That's the trick, right there. Our circle only goes as wide as we draw it. Draw it at "my team" and the other guy is on his own. Draw it at "human" and nobody is.

In the summer of 1954, at a camp in Robbers Cave, Oklahoma, researchers split a group of eleven-year-old boys into two teams and turned them against each other with competitions until they were raiding cabins and burning each other's flags. Then the adults staged a problem. On a camping trip, the truck that was supposed to go get everybody's food "wouldn't start." One of the boys suggested they get the tug-of-war rope, the same one the two teams had used against each other, and pull the truck. Both teams grabbed on. By the second pull they were all mixed together, and when the truck started, one boy shouted, "We won the tug-of-war against the truck!"

The truck was fine. The adults had staged the whole thing, so it's a story, not proof. What I like even more is what happened the year before. The same researcher tried this experiment at a camp in New York, and it fell apart. Those boys had gotten to know each other first. When the staff tried to stir up trouble, even pulling down a tent, the boys didn't turn on each other. They started suspecting the adults instead.

I'd bet that's who's doing the stoking, more often than not. Somebody, or something, that gains when we pull against each other. Follow it far enough and the gain is usually money.

Here's one piece of evidence. Researchers looked at about 2.7 million Facebook and Twitter posts from news outlets and members of Congress. Posts about the other side got shared about twice as often as posts about your own side. Every extra word aimed at the other side raised the odds of a share by 67%. Anger travels. And whoever owns the feed gets paid for every share.

The best defense? One of the best anybody has found is simply knowing the people on the other end.

The real evidence backs that up. A review of more than 500 studies found that contact between groups usually reduces prejudice. In India, young men were randomly assigned to cricket teams. Men who played *with* men from another caste made friends across caste lines. Men who only played *against* them ended up with fewer. Same game. The only difference was which way the rope was pointed.

In Iraq, after ISIS, Christian soccer players who were randomly given Muslim teammates were still choosing to practice with Muslims six months after the season ended. The researcher was honest about the limits: it didn't change much off the field. The hand over the wall changes how you see the person whose hand it was. Tearing down the whole wall takes a lot of hands.

But we've got the hands. People who study disasters for a living have found it for more than sixty years: when the worst happens, people mostly don't panic and don't turn on each other. They help.

Same rope. Different direction.

## 7. Sunrise

I promised I'd get to this. The middle isn't always right.

If one side says slavery is fine and the other says it's evil, the answer isn't "a little slavery." And if the middle were always right, anybody could move it just by standing farther out. Pull harder on your end and the "center" slides toward you. That's not balance. That's a game, and the people at the edges know exactly how to play it.

So let me be clear about what Tension is.

About 2,300 years ago, Aristotle wrote about courage. He said it sits between two failures: cowardice, the man who runs from everything, and recklessness, the man who charges into everything. But he was careful about it. Courage isn't the halfway point. It's the *right* point, and finding it takes judgment. He used a wrestler to explain. If ten pounds of food is too much and two is too little, a good trainer doesn't automatically order six. Six might be too little for a champion and too much for a beginner. The right amount depends on who's eating.

Then he wrote the line I keep coming back to. Some things have no middle at all. You can't get murder right by doing a moderate amount of it. Some things are just wrong.

And one more. The middle, done right, isn't lukewarm. When it comes to what's best and right, Aristotle called it "an extreme."

(He also defended slavery in another book, so I'm borrowing his idea, not his whole résumé.)

That's Tension. It isn't splitting the difference. It's keeping both pulls in your hands long enough to find the right point, using rules that don't change depending on which team brought the question. People first. Facts over tribe. Institutions earn trust or lose it.

It's harder than the ends. Aristotle knew that too. Anybody can get angry, he said, "that is easy." But doing it with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason and in the right way... "that is not for every one, nor is it easy." He also said to notice which way you tend to lean and pull yourself the other way, the way you'd straighten a bent stick.

I leaned one way for years. I'm still straightening the stick.

So here's what I'm asking, and it isn't much.

The next time you're sure about the other side, find one person on it. A real one, with a name and a face. Remember the atheists? People who actually knew one rated them about twenty points warmer. Getting to know someone on the other end is one of the most reliable things researchers have found.

Then ask them the question I've been asking myself for years. Take the parties out of it. What's NOT working for you? And listen to the answer like it's true. It probably is.

You won't agree on everything. You're not supposed to. That's the rope. The point isn't to win it. The point is to hold it, without dropping it and without yanking it out of the other guy's hands.

One last thing about the sun.

Remember how, at sunset, the sun is already gone by the time you see it sitting on the horizon? The air bends its light so you can still see it. The same thing happens in the morning, in reverse. At sunrise, you see the sun before it's actually up. The light gets to you first.

I think that's where we are. It's been a long, hard night. Nobody has to pretend otherwise. But look toward the horizon and you can already see some light. Most of us don't fit at either end. When disaster hits, people still mostly help each other.

We've been told the passion lives at the ends of the rope. It doesn't. It lives in the middle, where both pulls meet, where the night meets the day.

Everybody spends their life trying to relieve tension.

I'm asking you to go find it.
